## Core argument

### Work does not need a building

The commute between Chicago and St. Louis makes the point directly. The work does not happen in the building. It happens on the machines, in the workflows, in the agents that keep running when you are not there.

The desk is where you sit. The work is distributed across the fleet. The location is almost incidental.

This is not a remote-work argument. It is a distributed-work argument. The human can be in a coworking space, at home, at a desk in a different city, and the work continues because the work is not tied to the human's location. It is tied to the machines and the network and the standards.

### Tailscale as the boring glue

The reason the remote agents are usable is not that they are impressive. It is that the network connects them securely and boringly. Tailscale is the glue.

It gives the machines a private network they can use regardless of where they are physically. It does not expose management ports. It does not require a VPN that breaks when you change locations. It just works, and that is the point.

The infrastructure that matters is the infrastructure you do not have to think about. Tailscale is in that category. When the network is boring, the work is not interrupted by the network.

### Infrastructure over aesthetics

The workstation setup is not about looking good on camera. It is about running reliably. The mini PCs, the switch, the display plane, the shared monitor, the LAN — all of it is chosen for function, not presentation.

The system is inspectable because it has to be. The work is real because it has to survive the human walking away from the desk.

This is the aesthetic of the small factory. It is not beautiful in the way a clean desk photo is beautiful. It is beautiful in the way a working machine is beautiful — because it does the thing it was built to do, consistently, without drama.

### What "going to work" means when the team is software

When the team is software, going to work does not mean commuting to a building. It means connecting to the network, opening the control surface, and checking what the agents did while you were gone.

The day starts with a review of the night's work, not with a commute. The human arrives at the work, not at a place.

This is the practical form of Human 2.0. The human is not less present. The human is present in a different way — as the supervisor, the reviewer, the person who sets the standard and signs off on the day's output.

## Working example / proof case

The operator is in Chicago. The machines are in St. Louis. The operator connects to the network. The operator opens the control surface. The operator reviews the night's work. The operators accepts some of it. The operator rejects some of it. The operator sends back the rejections with instructions. The agents revise. The operator reviews again.

The operator did not commute. The operator did not go to a building. The operator arrived at the work.

## What can go wrong

- **Failure mode:** The operator cannot reach the machines because the network is not set up correctly.
- **Why it happens:** The operator treats the network as an afterthought. The operator does not invest in the boring infrastructure.
- **How to detect it:** The operator cannot reach the machines from a different location. The operator has to be in the same room as the machines to do the work.
- **How the human responds:** The operator invests in the boring infrastructure. The operator makes the network boring. The operator makes the work not depend on the location.

## Why this matters in 2026

The geography of work is changing. The people who cling to the office-as-place model will pay for the machinery of commuting and presence. The people who treat the office as a set of machines, a network, and a standard will be able to work from wherever the desk and the power and the Wi-Fi are.

The small factory travels.

## Counterargument / skeptical reader

A critic would say that the office provides things that a desk and a network cannot provide: collaboration, culture, mentorship, the energy of a team. That is true for some kinds of work. For the work the operator is doing — building, reviewing, directing — the office is not necessary. The work is on the machines. The team is software. The human is the supervisor.

The critic would also say that the operator is privileged to have a desk and a network and a swarm, and that most people do not have that option. That is a real concern. The book's position is that when the operator does have the option, they should take it. The small factory is available to anyone who can buy a few cheap machines and set up a network.

## Operator rule

Make the network boring. Make the work not depend on the location.

## Measurable test

Can the operator do the work from a different location? Can the operator reach the machines without a VPN that breaks? Can the operator review the night's work when they wake up? If the answer to any of those is no, the operator is still tied to the building.
